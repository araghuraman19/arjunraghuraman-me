The chemical engineering literature often uses a reparameterized Arrhenius expression, written around a reference temperature. As someone trained in organic and medicinal chemistry, I hadn’t come across it before. This article explains the advantages of the reparameterization (G. E. P. Box, “Fitting empirical data,” *Ann. N.Y. Acad. Sci.* 86, 792–816, 1960).

The example here is simulated so the true answer is known: Ea = 80 kJ/mol, k = 0.01 s⁻¹ at 70 °C, duplicate runs at 50, 60, 70, 80 and 90 °C. Observed rate constants were simulated as kᵢ = A exp(−Eₐ/(R Tᵢ)) + εᵢ, where the εᵢ are additive IID errors drawn from N(0, σ²). Fitted in the standard form, k = A·exp(−Ea/RT), with `NonlinearModelFit`, the estimates of A and Ea are correlated at **ρ = +0.99986** (the two sensitivity columns have cosine −0.99986). To four decimal places the data cannot tell a high-A, high-Ea pair from a low-A, low-Ea one.

## Highly correlated parameters

For a single response, least squares sees the parameters through the Jacobian J: one row per run, one column per parameter, each entry ∂k/∂θ at the optimum. Wolfram gives it symbolically in a couple of lines:

```wolfram
modelStd = A Exp[-Ea/(R T)];
modelRef = kr Exp[-(Ea/R) (1/T - 1/TR)];

D[modelStd, {{A, Ea}}] // Simplify   (* {k/A, -k/(R T)} *)
D[modelRef, {{kr, Ea}}] // Simplify  (* {k/kref, -k (1/T - 1/TR)/R} *)
```

In the standard form, the sensitivity to A is k/A and the sensitivity to Ea is −k/(RT). Their ratio, −A/(RT), is nearly constant over the experiment, because the absolute temperature only runs from 323 K to 363 K. So the two columns are nearly proportional. Normalized to unit length, they are **0.95° apart**:

The information matrix JᵀJ (with Cov(θ) ≈ σ²(JᵀJ)⁻¹) inherits this. Its eigenvalues measure how well the data determine each direction in parameter space. A tiny eigenvalue means a long axis of the confidence region, and the ratio of the largest to the smallest, the condition number, summarizes the problem.

### A note on scaling

Raw JᵀJ is not useful for this question. A is about 10¹⁰ s⁻¹ and Ea about 10⁵ J/mol, so the two columns differ by many orders of magnitude, and the raw condition numbers (5.5×10¹⁶ standard, 2.2×10¹³ centered) mostly reflect units. The columns have to be put on a common scale first, and this page uses two scalings:

- **Unit-length columns** (Fig. 2). JᵀJ becomes the matrix of cosines between the columns, `{{1, c}, {c, 1}}`, with eigenvalues 1 ± |c|. For the standard form, c = −0.99986 gives eigenvalues 1.99986 and 0.000137: **condition number 14,628**.
- **Relative sensitivities**, θⱼ·∂k/∂θⱼ (Fig. 3), which put both axes in "fractional change" units so the confidence regions can be drawn on the same footing. On this scaling the condition numbers are **2.6×10⁶ vs 5.2**.

Both scalings tell the same story. The numbers differ because they answer slightly different questions, so don't compare a number from one with a number from the other.

## Centering the temperature

Rewrite the model around a reference temperature:

k = k<sub>ref</sub>·exp[−(Ea/R)(1/T − 1/T<sub>ref</sub>)]

It's the same curve and the same fit quality, with k<sub>ref</sub> replacing A as the rate constant at T<sub>ref</sub>. The sensitivity of k to Ea (the second column of J) is now −k(1/T − 1/T<sub>ref</sub>)/R, which changes sign at T<sub>ref</sub>. The two columns are orthogonal when 1/T<sub>ref</sub> is the **k²-weighted mean of 1/T**.

The weights matter. With constant absolute error, the fast hot runs dominate the fit, so the optimal reference here is **360.3 K (87.2 °C)**, not the 70 °C midpoint. At 87.2 °C the condition number is exactly 1. At the midpoint it is still about 39:

## Results: same information, different shape

The relative-sensitivity JᵀJ has the **same determinant for both forms** (6.47×10⁻⁶). Going from (ln A, ln Ea) to (ln k<sub>ref</sub>, ln Ea) is a shear with unit determinant, so the total information the data carry, which corresponds to the area of the confidence region, is unchanged. What changes is the shape. A thin diagonal needle with axes in the ratio **1601 : 1** becomes an axis-aligned ellipse at **2.3 : 1**:

## Takeaway

The A–Ea correlation is a property of how the model is written, not a shortage of data. The Jacobian shows it (two nearly parallel columns), the eigensystem of JᵀJ names the unresolvable direction, and a reference temperature at the k²-weighted center of 1/T removes it while leaving the information content untouched.

In practice this is relevant beyond Arrhenius reparameterization. A well-conditioned parameterization makes `NonlinearModelFit` converge faster and more reliably from rough starting values.
